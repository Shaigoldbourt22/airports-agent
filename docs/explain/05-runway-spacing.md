# 5. Runway spacing, and why it matters

This is the one piece of real aviation knowledge in the project, so it is
worth understanding properly.

Two runways side by side look like double the capacity. Often they are not.

Planes landing need to be kept apart. When the weather is clear, pilots can
see each other and controllers can run both runways at once. When cloud comes
in, controllers switch to instruments, and the FAA rules kick in:

- **Under 2,500 ft apart** — no independent approaches in poor visibility.
  Two runways start behaving like one and a half.
- **Under 1,200 ft apart** — they are worked as a **single runway**. Your
  arrival capacity roughly halves the moment it rains.

So an airport can have four runways on paper and the landing capacity of two.
Worse, this is the one problem money cannot fix. You can build a bigger
terminal, add gates, buy jet bridges. You cannot move a runway 1,000 feet
sideways without buying half a city.

That is why spacing is a scoring component, and why it is scored on the
published rule rather than normalised against peers: 100 if under 1,200 ft,
60 if under 2,500 ft, 0 otherwise. Normalising would make the least-bad
airport in a healthy group look like it is in crisis.
